\section{Problem Statement}
% #####################################################################

Automatic pelvic computed tomography (CT) analysis and volumetric segmentation depend on sufficiently annotated data. Metal artifacts can obscure anatomical boundaries near implants, motivating evaluation of segmentation robustness in this region. CTPelvic1K \citep{liu2021ctpelvic1k} includes a CLINIC-metal subset whose limited bone annotation constrains supervised evaluation. Its name does not establish that every case contains pelvic osteosynthesis, nor that other subsets are free of metal or foreign objects. The local audit will therefore determine implant types, eligible training anatomy and annotated evaluation cases through three-dimensional and multiplanar review. The magnitude of peri-implant segmentation degradation must be measured in this study rather than assumed from the dataset description.

While generative models based on diffusion processes excel at synthesizing visually plausible medical anomalies, they structurally fail when applied to metallic implants. State-of-the-art lesion synthesis models fall into two families, and neither represents the intensity changes that the synthesized object induces outside itself. Mask-bounded inpainting methods condition generation on a lesion mask: CLAIM \citep{ramzan2026claim} recomposes the background from the original image at every denoising step, DiffTumor \citep{chen2024tumorsynthesis} models nothing outside the mask, and LGESynthNet \citep{jacob2026lgesynthnet} is formulated as inpainting, although its latent compression still perturbs the whole slice incidentally rather than by design. Globally conditioned methods such as DiffBoost \citep{zhang2025diffboost} instead synthesize the entire slice from noise, using the mask only as an edge constraint. None of these works states an assumption of tissue non-rigidity; what they share is that no mechanism, and no training signal, accounts for object-induced intensity changes beyond the mask. This is physically incompatible with metal implants, which are rigidly geometric and produce severe attenuation profiles (beam hardening, photon starvation, and high-amplitude streaking) that propagate globally far beyond the implant's boundaries, destroying the signal of the surrounding soft tissue. \citet{deman1999} attribute such streaks to beam hardening, scatter, noise and the exponential edge-gradient effect, and show them radiating from the metal along the directions of highest attenuation; their simulation, however, uses an iron rod and dental amalgam in a two-dimensional fan-beam geometry, excludes non-linear partial volume, and reports no spatial extent, so the size of the affected peri-implant band remains unmeasured in the literature.

Physics-based metal artifact simulation provides a reference for appearance generation. This thesis adopts the hybrid simulation protocol of \citet{peters2025hybrid}, implemented with CatSim within XCIST \citep{deman2007catsim,wu2022xcist}, as its physical comparison protocol. Their phantom validation supports the evaluated simulation settings; it does not establish perfect physical coherence or validate pelvic osteosynthesis in three dimensions. Their training data use randomized metal placement, whereas their scoring benchmark uses expert placement. The unresolved placement problem is therefore scalable, anatomically constrained placement, rather than scalable metal insertion itself.

Even if automated placement were achieved, mere anatomical containment is insufficient; a valid simulation must reflect true surgical admissibility. Traditional deterministic surgical planning pipelines \citep{liu2025pipeline} attempt to optimize a single "perfect" trajectory. Yet real-world clinical data reveal a different reality. Grading iliosacral screws on a four-level cortical-breach scale, \citet{zwingmann2009navigated} report two distinct postoperative-CT distributions: grade~0 in 69\% of navigated placements (grades~1--3: 15\%, 8\%, 8\%) against grade~0 in 40\% of conventional placements (37\%, 11.5\%, 11.5\%). That scale reaches iliosacral fixation through \citet{smith2006iliosacral}, who apply it there, but it originates in the pedicle-screw literature: \citet{gertzbein1990} tabulate canal encroachment from the medial pedicle border in six 2~mm bins rather than four lettered grades, anchor the 2~mm cut to the epidural space, extrapolate the 4~mm cut from a single myelographic CT, and state no justification for the 6 and 8~mm cuts. The 2~mm bin width is therefore adopted here as a geometric convention, as with the 10~mm corridor criterion below, and not as a validated clinical threshold; its transfer from the spinal canal to the iliosacral corridor is an explicit assumption of this work. These are two surgical-technique populations that differ significantly, not one aggregate range; this work therefore targets the ordinal distributions themselves rather than a single imperfection rate. The distinction is not merely presentational. In a meta-analysis by the same first author, \citet{zwingmann2013} pool malposition rates of 2.6\% for conventional placement against 0.1\% under CT navigation, and the 2009 cohort enters that pool with zero events in both arms, because the included series record malposition only where a revision followed; the authors themselves note that no comparable grading systems are given across studies. Aggregate malposition rates and ordinal cortical-breach grades therefore measure different constructs, and only the latter is a usable target for a placement sampler. Sacral level also warrants separate treatment on anatomical grounds. Using multiplanar CT measurements, \citet{gardner2010safezones} report mean minimum iliosacral cross-sectional areas at S1 and S2 of 346 and 109.3~mm$^2$ in normal sacra, compared with 222 and 220.1~mm$^2$ in dysmorphic sacra. The approximately equal areas in the latter group support measuring each level on the individual anatomy rather than assuming a universal corridor-size hierarchy. These measurements concern an uninjured side and ideal central trajectories; they do not describe clinical malposition frequencies. In a retrospective clinical series, \citet{vandenbosch2002} reported postoperative neurologic complaints in 6 of 31 patients with the lower screw in S2, versus 1 of 49 with both screws in S1 ($p=0.01$). These are patient-level outcomes by screw configuration, not screw-malposition rates by level; the authors also identify a learning-curve bias associated with the change in placement practice. This clinical association supports retaining S1/S2 as a sampler variable, but supplies neither a causal level effect nor an ordinal cortical-breach prior for S2. Cortical breach by level need not follow the same ordering as patient-level neurologic outcome: on postoperative CT, \citet{herman2016} report breach in 36.5\% of S1 screws against 14.8\% at S2 ($p=0.035$), with 32\% of screws breaching overall and no screw lying entirely outside bone. The two findings are measured on different quantities and are not in conflict; the latter bounds the range of malpositions the sampler must be able to produce. The ordinal benchmark from \citet{zwingmann2009navigated} applies to S1; S2 placements are evaluated descriptively for geometry and breach grades without claiming clinical distribution matching. The premise that no canonical pose exists is not only inferred from surgical error rates; it is supported anatomically. Measuring the orientation of the bony surfaces that bound the corridor across seventeen cadaveric pelves, \citet{ziran2007fluoroscopic} report inter-specimen coefficients of variation of 7--25\% for most surfaces, rising to 43\%, 47--52\% and, for the orientation of the superior S1 ala in the sacral frontal plane, 97--140\%. A corridor that varies this much between individuals cannot be served by one fixed trajectory. Training a diagnostic network solely on "perfect" deterministic trajectories will inevitably cause it to fail when encountering the true variance of clinical errors. Therefore, a generative sampler must not optimize for perfection, but instead sample from the measured ordinal distribution of clinical malpositions, bounded by pelvic density representations \citep{arand2019pelvicring} and by osseous-corridor viability. For the latter, \citet{mclaren2021corridor} supply the operational element that a qualitative safe-zone description \citep{ramadanov2025safezone} does not: a reproducible corridor-diameter procedure over 433 pelves, yielding transiliosacral angular tolerances of $1.53 \pm 0.57^{\circ}$ at S1 and $1.02 \pm 0.33^{\circ}$ at S2. Their $\geq$10~mm corridor criterion is not an empirically validated safety threshold and is not treated as one here: \citet{kaiser2014dysmorphism}, from whom it is taken, describe it as a chosen conservative value and justify it dimensionally, as clearance of 1--2~mm around a 6.3--8~mm screw. It is therefore used as a geometric clearance convention rather than as an inherited clinical standard. \citet{kaiser2014dysmorphism} additionally supply what a corridor diameter alone does not: a reference frame computable on a CT volume, obtained by reformatting along the sacral axis perpendicular to the superior end plate of S1, with corridor angulation measured coronally against the line joining the iliac crests and axially against the line joining the posterior iliac spines, and a usable-length rule requiring at least 5~mm of clearance to the cortex on either side. The pose distribution sampled within that frame remains an explicit construction of this work.

\textbf{Recognized Gap.} Current attempts to bridge the metal-artifact domain shift are bottlenecked by three mutually exclusive paradigms: physical simulators do not supply scalable anatomically constrained placement, biological diffusion models cannot generate rigid global streaks, and deterministic surgical planners ignore the statistical reality of clinical malposition. To the best of the author's knowledge, there is no diffusion-based method that overcomes these barriers by combining: \textbf{(C1)} Non-biological rigid implant geometries, which avoid the threshold-dependent size distortion of fixed-HU metal segmentation (over-coverage in simulated slices, \citealp{xie2024implantsegmentation}; fragmentation of pelvic screws in the local audit); \textbf{(C2)} A surgically constrained placement sampler that accurately emulates the population distribution of clinical malpositions; and \textbf{(C3)} The transfer of multi-window HU encoding from artifact \emph{removal}, where it originates, to artifact \emph{synthesis}, coupled with a generation band ($B_{\delta}$) extended beyond the implant mask so that global streaking and beam hardening are modelled outside the metal itself. The multi-window framework is not claimed as novel here: it is established in metal-artifact-reduction work, and \citet{wang2025adaptiveweighting} attribute it in turn to earlier multiple-window learning. What is claimed is its use for generation, together with $B_{\delta}$, for which no published precedent quantifying a peri-implant band was found. This is the gap this work aims to fill.

Filling it rests on one assumption that the present literature neither grants nor refutes: that the \emph{appearance} of a metal artifact can be generated in the image domain, without access to projection data. The mechanisms involved are non-linear and are manipulated in the sinogram \citep{deman1999}, and dual-domain reduction networks report a penalty for operating on images alone: \citet{lin2019} obtain 31.45~dB PSNR from their image-domain variant against 33.51~dB for the full dual-domain network. Two qualifications keep that result from settling the question. Their image-domain variant is not blind to projection data, since it is fed a reconstruction of an already-interpolated sinogram, so the gap measures the value of refining the sinogram rather than of having projection access at all; and their task is artifact \emph{removal}, which they describe as ill-posed because information has been destroyed, whereas synthesis carries no such recovery requirement. This work therefore treats image-domain generability not as a premise but as part of what its distributional physical-coherence evaluation puts to the test, with the physics-based protocol of \citet{peters2025hybrid} supplying the mechanism-faithful reference against which the generated appearance is judged.

\section{Research Question}
% #####################################################################

What improvement does a surgical admissibility-constrained (bone density and cortical containment) 3D placement sampler, a multi-window Latent Diffusion Model (LDM) conditioned on the rigid implant geometries and an extended generation band ($B_{\delta}$), have in terms of distributional physical-coherence of the synthesized pelvic osteosynthesis implants and their local artifacts, when compared with both naive copy-paste insertion and the adopted physics-based simulation protocol of \citet{peters2025hybrid}? Downstream segmentation impact is deliberately excluded from this study and is stated as out of scope below.

% #####################################################################
\section{Hypothesis}
% #####################################################################

The synthetic generation of medical anomalies and metallic implants using a rigid geometry and extended spatial boundaries, HU-conditioned diffusion model and whose placement is surgically enforced via a density-based placement sampler is more physically-coherent than unconstrained generative baseline methods and on the same footing as the adopted explicit physics-based simulation protocol \citep{peters2025hybrid}. Placement coherence is quantified by Surgical Admissibility of Placement (SAP), the single metric introduced by this work, which emulates the measured ordinal distribution of real-world implant placement. Appearance coherence is quantified with the metrics of the adopted protocol under their published names, not with newly coined ones. Ablations reveal the non-redundant role of the multi-window encoding and of the extended boundary generation.

\section{Objectives}
% #####################################################################

\textbf{General Objective.} Design, implement and evaluate a physics-and-anatomy-aware latent diffusion pipline with rigid surgical constraints and extended HU-based conditioning, capable of generating physically coherent metallic implants intended, in future work, to improve the robustness of downstream segmentation networks.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Validation of a multi-window representation (Compulsory Go/No-Go):} Ensure HU reconstruction error (HU to multi-window to VAE to HU again) within the bone tissue, delimited by the same 150~HU bone threshold used by the adopted evaluation protocol \citep{peters2025hybrid,haneda2025aapm}, below a 25~HU threshold (Mean Absolute Error (MAE)). No published pass/fail threshold on CT-number accuracy was found, so this criterion is not taken from a source: it is anchored on the error level that current metal-artifact methods reach on the same scale. The NMAR algorithm that calibrates the community benchmark reports an RMSE of 20.2~HU, an image-domain diffusion model 12.3~HU \citep{karageorgos2024ddpm}, and a latent diffusion model 12.74~HU \citep{yun2026simulationdriven}. Because those values are RMSE while this criterion is MAE, and RMSE is never below MAE for the same residuals, the comparison fixes the order of magnitude rather than an equivalence; this is declared rather than resolved. This will serve as a guarantee that the intensity gradients that drive downstream applications are preserved.
  \item \textbf{Design of surgically constrained placement sampler:} propose position-orientation 3D pose of a parametric rigid screw model constrained by bone density maps \citep{arand2019pelvicring}, cortical boundaries with a 5~mm clearance rule, and osseous-corridor viability \citep{mclaren2021corridor}, expressed in the CT-computable reference frame of \citet{kaiser2014dysmorphism} (sacral-axis reformat; coronal and axial corridor angulation against iliac-crest and posterior-iliac-spine landmarks). Corridor geometry is measured on anatomical masks from TotalSegmentator 2.18.0 \citep{wasserthal2023}, which is built on nnU-Net \citep{isensee2021} (task \texttt{total}, default crop; robust crop as sensitivity analysis), quality-controlled per case and cleaned of connected components below 0.1\% of their structure, rather than on an HU threshold. The cited publication evaluates a 104-structure model and reports no accuracy for the S1 label used here, so its reported accuracy is not assumed for the version and classes used in this work. The sampler must yield a distribution of placements emulating the two measured ordinal malposition distributions of \citet{zwingmann2009navigated}, conditioned on surgical technique within S1, rather than a single artificial "optimized" trajectory. Sacral level remains a geometric conditioning variable, anatomically motivated by the segment-specific corridor measurements of \citet{gardner2010safezones}; S2 results are reported descriptively, because the clinical comparison of \citet{vandenbosch2002} concerns neurologic complaints by screw configuration and does not provide an ordinal S2 placement distribution. Sacral-morphology phenotypes are deliberately not used as a stratifying variable: their reported effect on safe-zone size is not consistent across studies, and the sampler instead measures corridor geometry directly on each volume.
  \item \textbf{Design of a conditioned synthesizer:} 2.5D ControlNet-guided Latent Diffusion Model (Stable Diffusion 1.5 backbone). A crucial detail in the formulation is that the synthesis mask must be extended with a spatial band $B_{\delta}$ ($\sim$12mm) over the implant boundary, to allow a more explicit manifestation of the streaking artifacts and beam hardening that occur outside of the implant mask in the generated image.
  \item \textbf{Formalization of the placement metric and adoption of an inherited appearance protocol:} Formulation of Surgical Admissibility of Placement (SAP) as the only metric introduced by this work, evaluated with Wasserstein-1 distances against the measured ordinal distributions. Appearance is evaluated with the metrics of the adopted protocol under their published names --- bone integrity, metal integrity and streak amplitude \citep{peters2025hybrid} --- and the previously proposed BFC and ISC are withdrawn, so that inherited measures are not renamed as contributions.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Explicitly out of scope.} Downstream segmentation evaluation (Dice, HD95) is excluded from this study. Two auditable conditions drive the exclusion rather than time alone: within the locally available cohort only a minority of the metal-bearing volumes carry verified bone annotation, and only part of the published collection is held locally, so a downstream result would rest on a cohort too small and too partially annotated to support the claim. The synthesis arms and the placement sampler are therefore evaluated on physical coherence and surgical admissibility alone; downstream utility is left as future work.

\section{Datasets}
% #####################################################################

\textbf{Primary Dataset.} CTPelvic1K \citep{liu2021ctpelvic1k} will be used as the source of patient anatomy and real artifact observations. The locally available dataset6 and dataset7 volumes are inventoried separately from the full published collection; the CLINIC-metal label alone does not establish implant type or annotation availability. \emph{Strict Isolation Rule.} No patient may contribute to both training and evaluation. The implant geometries consumed by the proposed sampler and by the physical simulation arm are parametric and are not derived from any evaluation volume. Duplicate-volume screening and patient grouping are required to enforce this separation.

\textbf{Implant geometry source.} Implants are modelled as parametric rigid screws rather than extracted from the metal-bearing volumes. Shaft diameter is drawn from the calibres reported for iliosacral fixation, 6.5--8.0~mm \citep{gardner2010safezones} and 6.3--8~mm \citep{kaiser2014dysmorphism}. Screw length is not taken from a fixed published range: it is bounded on each volume by the measured corridor under the usable-length rule of \citet{kaiser2014dysmorphism}. Features without a textual source in the reviewed literature (thread, cannulation, head and washer) are simplified to a smooth cylinder, and this simplification is declared as a limitation. The real implants of the metal-bearing subset do not enter the geometry bank; they serve as a descriptive contrast for placements, as an appearance reference for synthesis, and as a test--retest control where the same implant was imaged twice. The local audit motivates this choice: screws extracted from these volumes with a fixed HU threshold appear fragmented, with shaft diameters below the published calibres, and the extracted geometry changes between acquisitions of the same implant.

\textbf{Physical comparison protocol.} The hybrid simulation and evaluation protocol of \citet{peters2025hybrid}, also associated with the AAPM CT-MAR Challenge \citep{haneda2025aapm}, is adopted instead of an independently validated reimplementation of \BASESIM{}. Challenge data remain excluded from model training, and the scoring benchmark is reserved for final evaluation. Reproduction will document the code revision, configuration, reconstruction settings and access conditions. Both synthesis arms will use the same source anatomy and implant poses; a no-metal simulation/reconstruction control will quantify changes introduced by the physical arm alone. The published protocol is two-dimensional and evaluates MAR: extending it to pelvic osteosynthesis synthesis requires an explicit adaptation and does not inherit its validation automatically. RMSE and SSIM against the original CT will assess preservation outside the generation band, where changes are not intended; artifact realism within the affected region requires separate evaluation. No unverified numerical acceptance thresholds or MAR score calibration are adopted here.

\textbf{Field limitation: corridor geometry has only been characterized in intact pelves.} This is not a limitation of the present cohort but of the available literature. Every source that publishes sacral corridor geometry measured it in pelves without implants and without fracture: \citet{kaiser2014dysmorphism} excluded scans with implants obscuring the lumbosacral junction, \citet{mclaren2021corridor} used uninjured scans, and \citet{ziran2007fluoroscopic} worked on cadaveric specimens and warn that fracture, displacement and soft-tissue injury would alter what is visible. The reference frame adopted here rests on landmarks at the lumbosacral junction, which is precisely where streaking concentrates, so whether those landmarks remain locatable in metal-bearing volumes is not answered by any published source. It was therefore quantified on the local cohort. Landmarks were located by a heuristic on each volume, and a landmark region was counted as contaminated when its dark-streak or metal fraction exceeded the maximum observed in 69 metal-free patients of the same cohort. Among 65 patients with orthopaedic metal, all five landmarks were found within the field of view in 57. After a clinician reviewer (one surgeon, otorhinolaryngology), blinded to the automatic audit, confirmed the S1 level on every patient, the frame was computable in 48 patients and free of contamination at all five landmark regions in 29. Losses were driven more by field-of-view truncation of the iliac crests (7 patients) and by landmark localisation than by artifact contamination; in the 17 patients with a screening-detected iliosacral screw, the S1 level was correct in all and its region was contaminated in 3. The frame is therefore computed on metal-bearing volumes where it is computable; the handling of field-of-view-truncated cases is assessed separately, and the heuristic localisation of the iliac crests and posterior iliac spines has not been clinically reviewed.

\textbf{Corridor measurement on the local cohort.} Among 103 patients without osteosynthesis, 91 had a located S1 and 72 passed level quality control. Level discordance excluded 11 of 34 patients with non-orthopaedic objects and 7 of 57 clean patients, and one further patient was excluded because S1 reached the field-of-view border; this imbalance is declared. The maximum transsacral corridor diameter had a median of 9.5~mm (IQR 7.4--11.7), and the 10~mm convention was met in 29 of 72 patients with the default crop and in 27 with the robust crop; the crop is therefore reported as a measurement uncertainty that reaches the binary viability outcome. Component cleaning changed neither the transsacral corridor diameter nor its 10~mm viability in this cohort at any tested fraction up to 5\%; the fraction, like the occupancy rule below, was fixed after inspecting these results. Along the axis of the widest corridor, the median fraction of voxels at or below 150~HU inside the sacral masks was 0.42, so an HU-based bone definition would exclude much of the corridor. In metal-bearing volumes, where the segmentation labels the existing implant as bone, treating the implant as occupied space (local half-maximum threshold per object) lowered 10~mm viability from 53.8\% to 40.4\% in 52 patients with the default crop, and a fixed 2500~HU threshold, which thins the screws and therefore bounds the occupied corridor from above, gave the same proportion; these volumes are reported as a separate contrast and are not pooled with the corridor cohort.

\textbf{Local cohort audit.} Training eligibility requires manual review of three-dimensional views and multiplanar CT slices confirming absence of metal and foreign objects. HU screening only prioritizes this review, including cases from dataset6. Confirmed metal cases are candidates for testing, with pelvic osteosynthesis distinguished from other implants and external objects. Exact volume duplicates and repeated patients must be resolved before splitting. Final cohort sizes remain pending this audit.

\textbf{Why downstream evaluation is excluded.} The audit itself supplies the reason. Only a minority of the metal-bearing volumes carry verified bone annotation, and only a fraction of the published collection is available locally, so a Dice or HD95 result would rest on a cohort too small and too partially annotated to support a robustness claim. The metal-bearing subset used here is nonetheless the de facto clinical evaluation set for pelvic metal-artifact work, having been used as such in recent published work \citep{wang2025adaptiveweighting}. Where a clean reference image does not exist for these volumes, appearance realism is assessed against real artifact observations rather than against an unavailable ground truth.
